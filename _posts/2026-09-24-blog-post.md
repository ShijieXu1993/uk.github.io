While working on a task to recover the implied volatility surface for fixed-income futures options, I first needed to invert the Black formula to obtain the implied volatilities used to calibrate a SABR model. While exploring QuantLib, I found two different functions in Quantlib for doing this:

- `ql.blackFormulaImpliedStdDev()`
- `ql.blackFormulaImpliedStdDevLiRS()`

Both solvers return the implied standard deviation, $\sigma\sqrt{T}$, rather than the volatility, $\sigma$, itself. Consequently, neither function takes the time to maturity as an input. This is natural because volatility and time to maturity appear together as $\sigma\sqrt{T}$ in the Black formula.

Right off the bat, I tested it on several different cases. Interestingly, although the LiRS method performs slightly better overall, we can find examples showing that no method consistently dominates the others. A straightforward question, then, is: Do these experts have enough information to determine which method is better?

One of the main differences between the standard method and the LiRS method is the choice of initial guess. The standard method uses the Brenner–Subrahmanyam (1988) and Feinstein (1988) approximation for at-the-money (ATM) options.

### Theorem (Brenner–Subrahmanyam–Feinstein ATM Approximation)

Let $C$ be the price of an ATM European call option under the Black model, with forward price and strike satisfying $F=K$. Let $D$ denote the discount factor and let $\nu:=\sigma\sqrt{T}$ be the total standard deviation. For small $\nu$,

$$
\nu \approx \sqrt{2\pi}\,\frac{C}{D\,F}.
$$

Equivalently, the implied volatility is approximated by

$$
\sigma \approx \sqrt{\frac{2\pi}{T}}\,\frac{C}{D\,F}.
$$

#### Proof

When $F=K$, the Black formula gives

$$
\begin{aligned}
C
&=D\left[F N(d_1)-K N(d_2)\right] \\
&=D\,F\left[N\left(\frac{\nu}{2}\right)
             -N\left(-\frac{\nu}{2}\right)\right] \\
&=D\,F\left[2N\left(\frac{\nu}{2}\right)-1\right],
\end{aligned}
$$

where $N(\cdot)$ is the standard normal cumulative distribution function and $D$ is the discount factor. Its Taylor expansion around zero is

$$
N(x)=\frac{1}{2}+\frac{x}{\sqrt{2\pi}}+O(x^3).
$$

Substituting $x=\nu/2$ into the Black formula yields

$$
C
=D\,F\left[
2\left(\frac{1}{2}+\frac{\nu}{2\sqrt{2\pi}}+O(\nu^3)\right)-1
\right]
=D\,F\left[\frac{\nu}{\sqrt{2\pi}}+O(\nu^3)\right].
$$

Therefore, to first order,

$$
\nu \approx \sqrt{2\pi}\,\frac{C}{D\,F},
$$

which proves the approximation. In the spot formulation with negligible interest rates and dividends, $D\approx1$ and $F\approx S$, giving the familiar expression

$$
\sigma \approx \sqrt{\frac{2\pi}{T}}\,\frac{C}{S}.
$$

Away from ATM, QuantLib uses the Corrado–Miller extended-moneyness approximation.

### Theorem (Corrado–Miller Extended-Moneyness Approximation)

Let $V$ be the price of a European call or put option under the Black model, let $F$ be the forward price, let $K$ be the strike, and let $D$ be the discount factor. Define

$$
\eta=
\begin{cases}
+1, & \text{for a call},\\
-1, & \text{for a put},
\end{cases}
\qquad
m=\eta(F-K),
\qquad
A=\frac{V}{D}-\frac{m}{2}.
$$

The Corrado–Miller approximation to the implied total standard deviation $\nu=\sigma\sqrt{T}$ is

$$
\boxed{
\nu
\approx
\frac{\sqrt{2\pi}}{F+K}
\left(
A+\sqrt{A^2-\frac{m^2}{\pi}}
\right)
}.
$$

Equivalently, the implied volatility is approximated by

$$
\boxed{
\sigma
\approx
\frac{\sqrt{2\pi}}{(F+K)\sqrt{T}}
\left(
A+\sqrt{A^2-\frac{m^2}{\pi}}
\right)
}.
$$

This expression extends the Brenner–Subrahmanyam–Feinstein approximation to options away from the money. Indeed, when $F=K$, we have $m=0$ and $A=V/D$, so the formula reduces to

$$
\nu\approx\sqrt{2\pi}\,\frac{V}{D\,F}.
$$

The approximation requires $A^2-m^2/\pi\geq0$. In its implementation, QuantLib replaces a negative value of this radicand with zero.

Essentially, the standard method is using linear terms to approximate the exponential form appeared in the Black formula.  The approximation performs well when the inplied standard deviation is relative small.

The LiRS method comes from BBG quant Minqiangn Li, and Dan, Stefanica and Rados Radoicic from Baruch college. In this blog we only pay attention to the initial guess of the implied standard deviation, which is the RS part.

Instead of using linear approximation, RS method uses the Pólya approximation, which is an explicit approximation of the standard normal cumulative distribution function:

$$
N(x)\approx
\frac12\left[1+\mathrm{sign}(x)\sqrt{1-e^{-2x^2/\pi}}\right].
$$

By replacing the standard normal cumulative distribution function with the analytically invertible Pólya approximation, it transforms the Black pricing equation into an algebraic expression with low- and high-volatility branches, selecting the appropriate branch according to the observed option price. Compared with simpler ATM-based approximations, the RS method generally remains more accurate across a wider range of moneyness, maturities, and volatility levels. Although it is not an exact implied-volatility solution, it is especially valuable as a robust initial guess for an iterative method such as LiRS, improving convergence speed while retaining the accuracy of the final numerical inversion.

The following chart compares the accuracy of the standard QuantLib initial approximation and the Radoičić–Stefanica (RS) approximation across the feasible strike–market-price plane for a European call option. The forward is fixed at 100, the time to maturity at one year, and the risk-free rate at zero. At each feasible point, both approximations are compared with a high-accuracy numerical solution of the Black implied standard deviation. Red regions indicate that the standard approximation is more accurate, while blue regions indicate that the RS approximation is more accurate. White areas represent prices outside the no-arbitrage bounds or points where a valid implied standard deviation cannot be calculated.

The RS approximation provides the more accurate initial estimate across nearly the entire feasible strike–market-price region, outperforming the standard approximation at approximately 99.2% of valid points. The standard approximation performs better only in a few narrow regions, mainly close to the ATM and the boundary whose implied vol is extremely low. **Overall, the result supports using the RS approximation as a more reliable initial guess for calculating Black implied standard deviation.**
